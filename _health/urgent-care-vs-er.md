When you feel unwell, the first decision is often the hardest: where do you go? Choosing the right setting gets you the right level of care, usually faster and at lower cost — and it keeps emergency departments free for people who need them most.

<div class="callout warn" markdown="1">
**Call your emergency number or go to the emergency department now** for: chest pain or pressure; trouble breathing; signs of stroke (face drooping, arm or leg weakness, slurred speech); severe bleeding; a seizure; fainting or unresponsiveness; a severe allergic reaction with swelling of the face or throat; a serious head injury; severe burns; sudden, severe pain; or thoughts of harming yourself or others.
</div>

## The emergency department

Emergency departments (ER or A&E) are staffed and equipped for life-threatening and limb-threatening problems around the clock. Patients are seen in order of how serious their condition is, not when they arrived, so people with minor problems can wait many hours.

If you think someone is having a heart attack or stroke, call an ambulance rather than driving. Paramedics can start treatment on the way and alert the hospital.

## Urgent care centers

Urgent care is for problems that need attention today but are not life-threatening. Many centers offer X-rays, basic blood and urine tests, stitches and splints. They commonly treat:

- Sprains, strains and possible simple fractures
- Minor cuts that may need stitches, and minor burns
- Fevers, flu-like illness, sore throats and ear pain
- Urinary symptoms and mild skin infections or rashes
- Mild asthma flares that respond to your usual inhaler

Waits are usually much shorter than in an emergency department, and costs are typically lower. If urgent care decides you need emergency treatment, they will send you on.

## Telehealth, walk-in clinics and pharmacists

**Telehealth (video) visits** suit many minor illnesses, rashes, medication questions, refills and mental-health follow-ups. **Walk-in and retail clinics** handle simple infections, vaccines and minor ailments. **Pharmacists** can answer medicine questions and, in many countries, treat minor conditions or provide emergency supplies of regular medicines.

## Your primary care doctor

Your own doctor or practice is the best place for ongoing conditions, checkups, screening, new symptoms that are not urgent, and referrals to specialists. They know your history, and continuity with one clinician is linked to better care. Many practices offer same-day appointments or a nurse line — call them first if you can.

## A quick comparison

| Setting | Best for | Typical wait | Relative cost |
|---|---|---|---|
| Emergency department | Life-threatening problems | Varies by severity | Highest |
| Urgent care | Same-day, non-life-threatening | Often under an hour | Moderate |
| Telehealth | Minor illness, refills, advice | Often same day | Low |
| Primary care | Ongoing and non-urgent care | Days to weeks | Low to moderate |
| Pharmacist | Medicine questions, minor ailments | Minutes | Lowest |

## Children are different

Babies and young children can become unwell quickly. Seek urgent medical advice for a baby under 3 months with a temperature of 38°C (100.4°F) or higher, and emergency care for any child who is floppy, hard to wake, breathing fast or with difficulty, has a rash that does not fade when pressed, or shows signs of dehydration such as no wet diapers.

## When you are not sure

Call a nurse or health advice line if one is available to you — for example NHS 111 in England, 811 in most Canadian provinces, healthdirect in Australia, or the nurse line on many insurance cards. Our [care navigator](/find-care/) can also suggest the right setting in about 30 seconds.

Photo ID, your insurance or health card, a list of medicines and allergies, and a short note of when symptoms started. Our free [visit prep builder](/tools/visit-prep/) creates a one-page summary you can print or show on your phone.
