Blood tests are among the most common medical tests. Understanding what they measure makes it easier to ask good questions — but always review results with the clinician who ordered them.

## Complete blood count (CBC)

- **Hemoglobin and hematocrit** measure your blood's oxygen-carrying capacity. Low values can mean anemia.
- **White blood cells** help fight infection. High counts can occur with infection or inflammation; low counts have many causes.
- **Platelets** help blood clot.

## Basic or comprehensive metabolic panel (BMP or CMP)

- **Glucose:** blood sugar. A fasting result of 100–125 mg/dL (5.6–6.9 mmol/L) suggests prediabetes and 126 mg/dL (7.0 mmol/L) or higher on two tests suggests diabetes.
- **Electrolytes** such as sodium and potassium, which affect nerves, muscles and heart rhythm.
- **Kidney function:** creatinine and eGFR (estimated glomerular filtration rate).
- **Liver tests** (in the comprehensive panel): enzymes such as ALT and AST, plus bilirubin and albumin.

## Lipid panel (cholesterol)

Measures total cholesterol, LDL (“bad”) cholesterol, HDL (“good”) cholesterol and triglycerides. Your target depends on your overall heart risk. See our guide to [cholesterol](/health/cholesterol/).

## Hemoglobin A1C

Shows your average blood sugar over about three months.

| A1C | What it suggests |
|---|---|
| Below 5.7% | Normal |
| 5.7% to 6.4% | Prediabetes |
| 6.5% or higher | Diabetes (usually confirmed with a repeat test) |

## Other common tests

- **TSH** (thyroid-stimulating hormone) checks thyroid function.
- **Vitamin B12, folate and ferritin** help investigate tiredness and anemia.
- **Urinalysis** looks for infection, blood, protein and sugar in urine.

## Why reference ranges vary

Each laboratory sets its own reference range, usually covering about 95% of healthy people. That means about 1 in 20 healthy people will have a result just outside the range for any single test. Ranges can also vary by age, sex and pregnancy. A slightly abnormal result often needs only a repeat test or nothing at all — your clinician will interpret it in context.

## Preparing for a blood test

Ask whether you need to fast (some glucose and lipid tests require 8–12 hours without food), whether to take your usual medicines, and when results will be ready. Drink water unless told otherwise — it makes drawing blood easier.

## Questions to ask about your results

- Which results are outside the range, and does that matter for me?
- How do these compare with my previous results?
- Do I need a repeat test, more tests, or any treatment?
- How can I get a copy of my results?
