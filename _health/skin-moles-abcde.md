Most moles and skin spots are harmless. But skin cancer is very common, and melanoma — the most serious type — is far more treatable when found early. A monthly self-check takes five minutes.

## The ABCDEs of melanoma

- **A — Asymmetry:** one half does not match the other.
- **B — Border:** ragged, notched or blurred edges.
- **C — Colour:** several shades of brown or black, or patches of red, white or blue.
- **D — Diameter:** larger than about 6 mm (the size of a pencil eraser), although melanomas can be smaller.
- **E — Evolving:** changing in size, shape, colour or height, or new symptoms such as itching, crusting or bleeding.

Also watch for the **“ugly duckling”** — a spot that looks different from your other moles.

## How to check your skin

1. Use a full-length mirror and a hand mirror in good light.
2. Check everywhere, including your scalp, palms, soles, between toes, under nails and genital area.
3. Ask a partner to check your back, or take photos so you can compare over time.

## Other reasons to see a dermatologist

- A sore or spot that does not heal within a few weeks
- Acne that is not responding to over-the-counter treatment or is leaving scars
- Eczema or psoriasis that is hard to control
- Sudden or patchy hair loss, or nail changes
- Persistent itching, rashes or hives

In some health systems you will need to see primary care first for a referral. Your clinician can arrange an urgent referral for a suspicious spot.

<div class="callout warn" markdown="1">
**Seek urgent care** for a rapidly spreading red, hot or painful area of skin, especially with fever, or a widespread blistering rash. Call your emergency number for a rash with swelling of the face, lips or tongue, or difficulty breathing.
</div>

## Sun protection that works

- Use broad-spectrum sunscreen with SPF 30 or higher, applied generously and reapplied every two hours and after swimming or sweating.
- Seek shade during the middle of the day, and wear a wide-brimmed hat, sunglasses and covering clothes.
- Avoid tanning beds.
